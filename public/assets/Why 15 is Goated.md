If there's a tier list for all the time signatures, 15/16 is definitely at the top. 

It is usually interpreted as: 4443, 333, and 55555. I would argue that 4443 is absolute gold in the space of prog music involving any sort of metric modulation. I categorize metric modulation into two categories: by subdivision and by pulse. 

By subdivision is where you simply change the subdivision of the pulse or stick to the current subdivision then interpret a new pulse on top of that. A easy example is Para Mexer by Animals As Leaders at 2:36. Before this, we have BPM X with subdivisions of 4 (or 16th notes). However, if we change the subdivision to 3 (or triplet-eighth notes), then treat it as the new subdivision-of-4, then we get the new pulse, where its BPM is 0.75 X. 

By pulse is when the underlying subdivision/tactus does not change. For example, the intro to Lights Out by Car Bomb re-interprets the pulse based on 6 ->  5 -> 4 -> 3. The modulations done in The Sentinel is the same mechanic. 

There's another way of doing metric modulation that is rather uncommon. It is not really related to the discussion about 15 but, an example is Monomyth by Animals as Leaders. Watch the [offical breakdown](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1z3R5DgigLA) if you havn't. The beginning part is clearly modulation by pulse, where the 23 223 223 23 23 223 is interpreted between subdivision-of-8 (32nd notes) and of-6 (6-tuplets). However, the interesting part is at 1:34, where the 2 + 3 become 3 + 5. If we consder both subdivisions to be subdivision-of-4, then the BPM shifts from 112.5 to 178, which is a weird ratio. How they do it is quite brutal: the first 2+3 added up = 36; if we keep the original pattern and apply 3+5, then the toal becomes 57. Then we take the ratio of 57:36, which is 1.583333..., and multiply this by 112.5, we get about 178. So it is modulation by-hypermeter. I do not understand how their brain process this when performing. Then at 1:53, we get a new tempo where the subdivision is of-3 (or triplets). The BPM is 87. How this happens is also kind of weird: to avoid the weird number of 57, they decided to cut off the last part of 335 in the groove to 334, making it 56 instead of 57. So, rather than doing 57:36, we now do 56:36 and get 1.55555... Then we apply this ratio back to 56, we get roughly 87. I'm proud to figure this out since Matt forgot how he did this math already, lol. After this part, however, when going back to the 6-tuplet groove, the BPM changes back to 150; they were probably tired of keep modulating and just decided to go back to the original tempo. Totally understandable.

And back to the topic. There is something weird that metric modulation by-subdivision can do that by-pulse can't easily do. Normally after a modulation, the modulated subdivision AND meter's length are both proportionate to the original subdivision and meter. For example, in the Para Mexer example, both the new subdivision and meter length were 0.75 of the original. But what if the meter's sub-grouping isn't constant? For example, if we have a meter of 7, grouped as 43 (another common grouping is to double it and do 4433), then how will we modulate by-subdivision here? For example, going to 5, which is grouped as 32 (or 3322)? We have three choices here: we can make the 3 into a 2, so the ratio is 3:2 or 1.5, or we can make the 4 into 3, so the ratio is 1.3333..., or we can take the easy answer and take the meter as a whole and do 7:5. However, I think it loses the fun. Basing the modulation off of 4 and 3 is much more interesting. But that's also when we run into the problem: if we do the fun approach,  because the ratio of 3->2 and 4->3 is different, we don't really get 32 or 3322. So usually, in bands like Victoria (duo by Joshua De La Victoria and Matt Garstka), they choose one of the ratio and apply the *same* ratio to the other. So it is effectivly the whole bar doing 3:2 or 4:3 rather than doing crazy stuff with the subgrouping individually. Something interesting happens here because the length of the total measure is no longer proportionate to the original; you can't just scale the original to this without a very complex and weird decimal or fraction. 

The point is, if you do the normal modulations, the meter length is almost always transformed by a simple ratio. This is important because you can't really jump out of the original BPM unless you do some crazy hypermeter modulation like Animals as Leaders. You are in constant of a simple ratio-relationship to the original BPM; it is always multiplication or division (with a simple ratio). But with this, you can basically do add/minus to the BPM, which unlocks a lot more freedom. And now, finally comes to the argument for 15. 

As I said in the beginning, 4443 is the key reason. You can now see why it's valuable for metric modulation: its structure is uneven (a 3 with three 4s). There are some other numbers that does similar stuff, like 7 -> 2221, 9 -> 22221, 11 -> 3332, etc. However, we must remember 4/4 is very special,  *the default* when it comes to western music, and 15/16 sounds very close to it. Take Eikasia by Mestis for example, it just sounds awfully natural. I bet average listeners might not even notice that it's not in 4/4. There's also something like 17, which is usually grouped as 4445, where 5 can be further divided into 23 or 32. However, 44423 often sounds kind of like 4442 with an added 3, as the accent of the 3 is placed on the 15th note. If you do 44432, then it just sounds like 15 but with an extra 2. It is hard to do 44441, because the 1 will stand out very obviously. You can also try to keep 5 as itself, no further grouping. But any drummer who's tried to keep a 5 intact next to a string of 4s knows why it easily collapses because 4 is often grouped as 22. Also I would argue that taking 1 out (4 -> 3) is almost always easier/more-natural than adding one in (4 -> 5). 19 shares this similar issue, where the common grouping is 44443. An example is Celestial Terrestrial Commuters by Mahavishnu Orchestra, and you can very clearly how the last 3 sounds like an something extra to a 4/4. Therefore, the structure of 4443 has an natural advantage as it is close to 16 (4444). And the issues with grouping 7 and 9 as 2 and 1 is that 1 is easily absorbed into other sub-groups, making it 223 or 232 or something like that. It is hard to treat 1 as it's own subgroup without a further subdivision. This is why things like 43 or 322 is much more common for 7. 4433 is another good competitor but ultimately it's further from 16 than 4443. 

Continue with 15. I will use Kepler by Victoria as the example here. We start off with 11, grouped by 3332. Then they do 3332 -> 4443, modulate by-hypermeter. The original BPM is 176, then do 15:11, we get 240, which is the new BPM. But the great thing about 15 is how it is both three 5 and five 3, which is what this song played around with. There's a 5:3 polymeter starting at 4:26 of the song, and sticks to 555 after that. But then, they modulated to 333 at 5:11, then briefly 777 (drums) at 5:55, come back to 333, then 555 starting at 6:18. You can use 555 to modulate to any 3-number patterns, which means 6 (222), 9 (333), 12 (444), etc., and similar case for 33333. And yet, 15 has the 4443 part as the uneven structure, allow dis-proportionate-modulation, so it can really act as the middle bridge to take you to anything. Another useful way to use 15 is just simply adding a subdivision or remove one from 16; the amount of things you can play around with this is really endless.
